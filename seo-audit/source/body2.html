Section 1

How to read this report

This replaces the first audit. That one was written without access to your website, so half of it was "go and check this". You then sent the real data: a Screaming Frog crawl of 475 URLs, four Search Console exports, your live robots.txt file, and a Core Web Vitals screenshot. Everything in this report is now measured. No guessing is left.

What each issue looks like

Every issue has the same five parts: the evidence (a number from your own crawl), why it matters in plain words, the fix as numbered steps, a check you can run yourself to prove it worked, and the hours it should take.

Who does the work

Four issues need a developer. Five can be done by anyone who can log into WordPress. Three are writing jobs for the marketing team. Each card says which.

The one picture that explains everything

Think of Google as a visitor with a stopwatch. It gives your website a fixed amount of time per visit. In that time it wants to walk into every room and write down what is inside.

Right now, three things are happening:

  • It keeps walking into cupboards. 74 of your web addresses are doorways that immediately push it back to the front door. It spends its time on those instead of your real pages.
  • The rooms take too long to open. Your pages carry roughly ten times more code than they need, so each one is slow to load.
  • Many rooms have no label. 87 of your pages have no description, 9 have no headline, and 10 different pages are all called "Featured".

The result is in your Search Console: 131 pages that Google has seen and decided not to put in its index. They are not penalised. They are simply not worth the trouble yet. This report makes them worth the trouble.

The diagnosis

Twelve issues, but only three root causes. Fix the roots and most of the twelve disappear together.

ROOT 1
Google's time is being spent on pages that are not pages

Out of 475 URLs in your crawl, 86 are redirects — addresses that exist only to bounce the visitor somewhere else. 74 of those are date archives such as /blog/2026/08/19/, and every single one dumps Google back on your homepage.

Search Console confirms it from Google's side: 72 pages classified "Page with redirect". On a site with only 147 real pages, more than a third of everything Google touches is a dead end. That is the single clearest reason 100 genuine pages sit in "Crawled – currently not indexed".

Fixed by issues 1, 5, 10 and 11.

ROOT 2
Every page carries about ten times the code it needs

Your average page sends 343 kilobytes of raw HTML before a single picture loads. A well-built page of the same content sends 30 to 60. The About page sends 570 KB. On top of that sit 90 separate stylesheet files and 13.4 MB of images, 24 of which are over 100 KB each and the largest of which is 1.3 MB.

Why so heavy: the crawl shows four page-building systems loaded at once — The7 theme, Elementor, Elementor Pro, and WPBakery "Ultimate VC Addons" — plus Revolution Slider, a pop-up plugin and a translation plugin. Each one insists on loading its own styles on every page, whether that page uses it or not.

This is what produces the 4.9-second Largest Contentful Paint in your Search Console. Fixed by issues 3 and 4.

ROOT 3
Pages are not labelled, so Google cannot tell them apart
Missing description87/147

Ten pages share the title "Featured" and the headline "Category Archives: Featured". Four more pages duplicate categories that already exist at another address. When Google meets ten identical labels it keeps one and discards nine. Fixed by issues 5, 6, 7 and 8.

Section 4

The 12 critical issues

01
74 date addresses that throw Google back to the homepage
Developer · 2–3 hours · Critical
Evidence: the crawl found 74 addresses in the shape /blog/2026/08/19/, /blog/2023/01/12/ and so on. Every one answers "301 Moved Permanently" and sends the visitor to https://marketeersresearch.com/. Search Console counts 72 of them under "Page with redirect". They exist because each blog post prints its publication date as a clickable link, and your theme then redirects that date page to the homepage.

Why it matters. Google arrives to read your articles. Instead it finds 74 doors that open onto the front step. It learns nothing, and it uses up the visit. This is the largest single drain on your site, and it is also the easiest to stop.

1

Log in and find the theme settings

Go to marketeersresearch.com/wp-admin and sign in. In the black menu on the left, look for The7. Click it, then click Theme Options.

2

Turn off the date under blog posts

Inside Theme Options, open the Blog section, then Post Meta (sometimes called "Post details" or "Meta information"). You will see switches for Author, Date, Categories, Comments. Switch Date off. Click Save.

If your posts are built with Elementor instead, edit one post, click the block showing the date, open Meta Data in the left panel, and remove Date from the list. Repeat on the blog listing template under Templates → Theme Builder.

3

Prove the links are gone

Open any blog post. Right-click the page and choose View page source. Press Ctrl+F and search for /blog/2026/. You should now find zero results. Before the fix you would find one or two on every post.

4

Send the leftovers somewhere sensible

Google will keep checking those 74 addresses for a few weeks. Right now they point at the homepage, which is the wrong answer to "show me August 2026". Ask your developer to change the redirect target from the homepage to https://marketeersresearch.com/blog/. It is usually one line in the theme's functions file or a rule in the Redirection plugin.

5

Tell Google to stop looking

Open Search Console → Pages → Page with redirect → click Validate Fix. Google will re-check over the next two to four weeks and the count should fall from 72 towards zero.

How you know it worked: the "Page with redirect" number in Search Console drops below 10, and a fresh Screaming Frog crawl shows fewer than 15 redirects in total instead of 86.
02
Ten category pages that take up to 9.1 seconds to answer
Developer · 3–5 hours · Critical
Evidence: your ten "Featured" listing pages are the slowest URLs on the entire site. Page 7 takes 9.11 seconds, page 4 takes 8.99s, page 8 takes 8.87s. Every other page on the site answers in about 0.78 seconds. This is server thinking time only, before any image or style has begun to download.
Normal page (median)0.78s
Featured page 27.58s
Featured page 79.11s

Why it matters. Google gives up on slow pages. Nine seconds is far past the point where it stops waiting, which is why these ten pages carry no value despite having 420 internal links pointing at the first one. It is also a warning sign: if one type of page can take nine seconds, your server can be made to struggle by anyone.

1

Confirm the cache is not covering these pages

You have WP-Optimize installed (the crawl shows its cache folder). In WordPress go to WP-Optimize → Cache. Look at the Advanced settings and find the list of URLs never to cache. Make sure nothing there matches /blog/category/ or page/. If a rule excludes paginated pages, delete that rule.

2

Turn caching on for these pages and warm it

Still in WP-Optimize → Cache: switch Enable page caching on, then turn on Preload (sometimes "Cache preloading") and set it to run daily. Preload means the server builds the slow page once, in the background, so no real visitor ever waits for it.

3

Show fewer articles per page

Go to Settings → Reading and set Blog pages show at most to 9 posts. Each of those "Featured" pages is currently building a dozen or more article previews, each with its own image and styling. Fewer previews means less work per page.

4

Have a developer look at the query

Nine seconds usually means the page is asking the database a slow question. Tell your developer exactly this: "The Featured category archive takes 9 seconds server-side. Please install Query Monitor, load /blog/category/blog/featured/page/7/ and send me the slowest database queries and the slowest plugin hooks." The usual culprits on this stack are Revolution Slider loading on archive pages and the related-posts logic in The7.

5

Clear the shield's memory

Your site sits behind Sucuri. After any speed change, log into Sucuri and click Clear cache, otherwise you will keep seeing the old slow version and think nothing happened.

How you know it worked: open httpstatus.io, paste https://marketeersresearch.com/blog/category/blog/featured/page/7/ and check the response time. It should be under 1.5 seconds. Run it twice; the second run should be under 0.5s once caching is on.
03
13.4 MB of images, and the 4.9-second paint they cause
Developer · 8–12 hours · Critical
Evidence: 151 images totalling 13.4 MB. 24 of them are over 100 KB. The five heaviest are a 1.30 MB cloud-storage background, a 1.27 MB team artboard, an 823 KB team photo, a 571 KB photograph and a 567 KB stock image. Only 4 of 151 use the modern WebP format; 142 are old-style JPEG and PNG. Search Console reports a Largest Contentful Paint of 4.9 seconds across a group of 27 blog URLs, rated Poor.

Why it matters. "Largest Contentful Paint" is simply the moment the biggest thing on the screen finally appears. Google wants that under 2.5 seconds. Yours is 4.9. On a Cairo or Riyadh mobile connection a 1.3 MB background image alone can take four seconds. Visitors leave before they read a word, and Google uses this measurement when choosing between you and a competitor.

1

Install one compression plugin

In WordPress go to Plugins → Add New, search for ShortPixel Image Optimizer, install and activate it. In its settings choose Compression type: Glossy, and switch on Also create WebP versions and Deliver WebP images. Glossy is the setting that saves most of the weight without a human being able to see the difference.

2

Run it over everything you already uploaded

Go to Media → Bulk ShortPixel and press Start. It will work through all 151 images. This takes an hour or two by itself, but you can close the tab and it keeps going. Expect the 13.4 MB to come down to roughly 3 or 4 MB.

3

Deal with the five monsters by hand

Compression alone will not save a 1.3 MB image that is 4000 pixels wide sitting in a 1200-pixel space. For each of these five, download it, resize it to 1600 pixels wide in any image editor, and re-upload:

  • cloud-storage-banner-background-remixed-from-public-domain-by-nasa… — 1.30 MB
  • Artboard-1teams.png — 1.27 MB
  • team.png — 823 KB
  • IMG-20221221-WA0183-1.jpg — 571 KB
  • data-information-report-statistics-strategy-concept-scaled.jpg — 567 KB

Note the two PNG files. PNG is for logos and flat graphics. Any photograph saved as PNG is roughly four times bigger than it needs to be. Re-save team.png and Artboard-1teams.png as JPEG.

4

Tell the browser to hurry up on the main picture

This one needs a developer. The big image at the top of each blog post is the thing being measured. Ask for two changes: "Add fetchpriority='high' and remove lazy-loading from the featured image at the top of single posts, and add a <link rel='preload'> for it in the page head." This alone typically removes a full second from the 4.9.

5

Make every other image wait its turn

Also in WP-Optimize, under Images, switch on Lazy load. This stops images far down the page from competing with the one the visitor is actually looking at. Make sure the very first image on a page is excluded from lazy-loading — that is what step 4 handles.

6

Measure, do not assume

Go to pagespeed.web.dev and test https://marketeersresearch.com/blog/the-role-of-ai-data-analytics-in-fmcg-growth-a-roadmap-for-revenue-optimization/ on the Mobile tab. Write down the LCP number before you start and after each step. You are aiming to go from 4.9s to under 2.5s.

How you know it worked: PageSpeed Insights shows LCP under 2.5 seconds on mobile, and Search Console's Core Web Vitals report moves that group of 27 URLs from "Poor" to "Good". Search Console updates on a 28-day rolling window, so give it a month before judging.
04
343 KB pages and 90 stylesheets, from four page builders at once
Developer · 6–10 hours · Critical
Evidence: median HTML size across your 147 pages is 343 KB; the About page is 570 KB, the homepage 511 KB, the blog index 499 KB. Every one of the 147 pages is over 200 KB. The crawl also found 90 separate stylesheet files, belonging to: Elementor (15), The7 theme (11), Elementor Pro (6), Ultimate VC Addons / WPBakery (4), a pop-up plugin (3), Contact Form 7, Revolution Slider and a Google translation plugin. The site is also being served over HTTP/1.1, the older protocol, which only allows a handful of files to download at the same time.

Why it matters. 343 KB of HTML for a page of text is like posting a letter inside a filing cabinet. The browser must read all of it before it can draw anything. Ninety stylesheets on the old protocol means the browser queues them six at a time. Together these are the reason issue 3's images arrive late.

1

Find out which builders are actually in use

Before removing anything, measure. Install the free Plugin Detective or simply ask your developer to search the database for WPBakery's marker. The practical test: open five or six pages in the editor. If none of them opens in WPBakery, then Ultimate VC Addons is loading its styles on every page for nothing.

Do this on a staging copy, not the live site. If you have no staging copy, install WP Staging first and make one. That is one hour well spent and it is what separates a calm fix from a broken website.

2

Remove what is not used

On the staging copy, deactivate in this order, checking the homepage and one blog post after each: Ultimate VC Addons, Revolution Slider, Google Language Translator. If a page breaks, reactivate that one and stop. Each removal typically takes 20 to 40 KB off every page on the site.

3

Switch on Elementor's modern loading

Go to Elementor → Settings → Features (older versions call it "Experiments"). Set Improved CSS Loading to Active, Inline Font Icons to Active, and Optimized Gutenberg Loading to Active. These make Elementor load only the styles a page actually uses. Save, then clear both the WP-Optimize cache and the Sucuri cache.

4

Combine what is left

In WP-Optimize → Minify, switch on Enable minify and Merge CSS files. Test the homepage, a service page and a blog post after saving. If anything looks wrong, turn Merge off and keep Minify on — minify alone is safe and still helps.

5

Turn on the modern protocol

Contact Sucuri support (or your host) with this exact sentence: "Please confirm HTTP/2 is enabled on the CloudProxy firewall for marketeersresearch.com." It is normally a switch on their side. You can confirm it yourself afterwards at tools.keycdn.com/http2-test.

6

Publish the staging changes to the live site

Only once every page on staging looks right. Take a full backup with UpdraftPlus first. Then push the changes live, clear both caches, and re-test the five pages.

How you know it worked: re-crawl with Screaming Frog. Median HTML size should drop below 200 KB and the stylesheet count below 40. PageSpeed Insights should no longer list "Reduce unused CSS" as a top opportunity.

09
Your robots.txt points at one sitemap and blocks another
Developer · 2–3 hours · High
Evidence: your live robots.txt advertises Sitemap: https://marketeersresearch.com/wp-sitemap.xml — the sitemap WordPress generates by itself — while also containing the line Disallow: /sitemap-custom-posts.xml, which forbids Google from reading a second sitemap. It also blocks /wp-json/. Search Console reports 4 pages "Blocked by robots.txt".

Why it matters. A sitemap is the map you hand to Google. You are handing over WordPress's automatic map, which lists everything including pages you never wanted found (see issue 10), while simultaneously padlocking a second map. Blocking a sitemap in robots.txt is never correct: either it should be readable or it should not exist.

1

Find out which sitemaps actually exist

Open each of these in a browser tab and note which loads: /wp-sitemap.xml, /sitemap_index.xml, /sitemap.xml, /sitemap-custom-posts.xml. If /sitemap_index.xml loads, your SEO plugin is making a better map and you should be advertising that one instead.

2

Pick one and only one

If the SEO plugin's sitemap exists, use it and turn off the WordPress one. If not, keep /wp-sitemap.xml and curate it in issue 10. Never advertise two.

3

Rewrite the file

Your SEO plugin has a robots.txt editor (Yoast: Tools → File editor; Rank Math: General Settings → Edit robots.txt). Replace the contents with this, changing the sitemap line to whichever you chose in step 2:

User-agent: *
Disallow: /wp-admin/
Allow: /wp-admin/admin-ajax.php
Disallow: /?s=
Disallow: /search/
Disallow: /?trk=
Disallow: /?clients=
Disallow: /?post_type=
Disallow: /?wordfence_logHuman=

Sitemap: https://marketeersresearch.com/wp-sitemap.xml

What changed: the line blocking a sitemap is gone, and the block on /wp-json/ is gone. /wp-json/ is the door WordPress itself uses; blocking it can break the editor and some plugins, and it protects nothing.

4

Find the 4 blocked pages before you assume they are fine

Search Console → Pages → Blocked by robots.txt. Click it to see the four addresses. If any is a real page you want found, the rewritten file above probably already unblocks it. If not, say which rule catches it and remove that rule.

5

Re-submit

Search Console → Sitemaps. Remove any old entries. Add the one sitemap you chose. It should report "Success" and a page count within a day or two.

How you know it worked: marketeersresearch.com/robots.txt shows the new file, Search Console's Sitemaps page shows one sitemap reading "Success", and "Blocked by robots.txt" falls to 0.

Section 7

Glossary

Crawl budget

The limited time Google spends on your site per visit. Spend it on dead ends and your real pages never get read.

Indexed

Written into Google's notebook. A page that is crawled but not indexed was read and rejected as not worth storing.

301 redirect

A permanent "we moved, go here" sign. Passes nearly all credit to the new address. Use these, not 302s.

Date archive

A page WordPress creates automatically listing everything published on one day. Almost never useful. Issue 1.

Canonical tag

A label inside a page saying "the official address for this is X". Yours are all correct.

LCP

Largest Contentful Paint: the moment the biggest thing on screen appears. Google wants under 2.5 seconds. Yours is 4.9.

H1

The main headline, marked as such in the code. One per page. Nine of your pages have none.

Meta description

The grey sentence under the blue link in Google. Does not affect ranking; strongly affects clicks.

robots.txt

A text file of house rules for search engines, and where you name your sitemap.

Sitemap

The map you hand Google listing pages worth visiting. It should contain only pages you want found.

Attachment page

A page WordPress makes for every uploaded image, containing only that image. Switch them off.

Orphan page

A page nothing links to. Google only learns of it from the sitemap, and usually ignores it.

Staging copy

A private duplicate of your website where changes can be tested without risk. Never remove plugins on the live site.

WebP

A modern image format, roughly 30% smaller than JPEG at the same quality. Only 4 of your 151 images use it.

HTTP/2

The modern way browsers talk to servers, downloading many files at once. Your site appears to still use the older HTTP/1.1.

Validate Fix

A button in Search Console telling Google to re-check a problem you have solved. Press it after every fix.
