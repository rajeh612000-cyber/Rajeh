Before the script

Three calls made against the brief

Each of these changes something the brief specified. Each is reversible, and the reversal is written out so nobody has to reconstruct it.

Naming

The product is Price Optimizer, not Smart Value

The brief called the subject "Smart Value Pricing Optimization." The company deck is more precise: Smart Value AI Solutions is the company, Price Optimizer is what clients buy, sitting beside Promotion Planner and AI-Enabled Market Research.

A viewer who wants this has to be able to ask for it by name, and "Smart Value Pricing Optimization" is not something they can ask for. The film is about one decision, so naming the one product that makes it is sharper than naming the company and leaving them to find the right door.

WasSmart Value runs the question before you commit.
→
NowPrice Optimizer runs the question before you commit.

Eight words either way. If house policy is to lead with the parent brand, revert this line and change nothing else.

Positioning

The end card uses the line you already own

Smart Value already says "Decision support, powered by AI. Predict before you decide," and the deck already says decisions are tested through simulation rather than analysed after the fact. That is the thesis of this film, which is a good sign.

So the end card carries the existing brand line. The earlier draft closed on "Before you change the price, simulate the decision," which is a serviceable line that duplicates yours at lower strength. It moves to the post caption. One line on screen, one in Mariam's mouth, same argument.

Proof

The 92% figure stays off the screen

The deck carries a real proof point: 92% confidence on an actual pricing recommendation, models validated against real market outcomes. It is the strongest asset available and I have deliberately kept the number off the end card.

A specific figure on screen for two seconds invites "92% of what, measured how" from exactly the people this film is aimed at, and there is no room to answer them. Unqualified it reads as marketing. Qualified it needs a sentence. So the end card carries the softer validation line and the number goes in the caption, where there is room and where the people who care enough to ask are already reading.

Sign-off required

Confirm the exact wording of the 92% claim with whoever owns it before it appears anywhere, caption included.

Camera

Shot list and angles

Only two on-camera setups in this film, so both have to work hard. The two-camera plan below is the single highest-value production decision on the day, because it is what makes the punch-in possible.

Two cameras
A-CAM  MCU, chest up  ·  ~50mm equiv  ·  f/2.8–f/4  ·  eye height or 1–2° below  ·  direct to lens B-CAM  CU, shoulders up  ·  ~85mm equiv  ·  25° off A-cam axis  ·  matched height

This gives the editor a clean cut anywhere without a jump cut. If only one camera is available, shoot every line twice at both sizes and match the performance.

Hook — 0:00

Start on A-cam, direct address. She is asking the viewer a question, so any off-axis eyeline turns it into an interview and the question stops being aimed at anyone.

Camera locked off, or a 2 to 3% push across the eight seconds. Imperceptible, but it adds forward pressure. No handheld. Handheld reads as vlog, and this film is asking for commercial authority.

The punch-in

Cut to B-cam on "Will it?". This is the most valuable two seconds of coverage in the film.

Drop-off concentrates around the five-second mark, and a hard size change right at that point resets attention without spending a word. Cut on the question, not after it.

Close — 0:49

A-cam, medium shot, waist up, direct to lens. Wider than the hook, same lighting, same background.

Consider one cut to B-cam on "Simulate it first," but only if the take supports it. If her delivery is strong, stay wide and let it play. A cut there can look like you did not trust the line.

Lighting

Soft key at about 45°, large source, short side. Keying the far side of her face from camera gives shape and reads more serious than flat frontal light. Background one and a half to two stops under.

Avoid a white cyc. This audience recognises white-cyc corporate video as an ad within a second and scrolls past it.

Background
& wardrobe

Real environment, defocused. Office or meeting room, not a branded backdrop. Nothing readable behind her head, and check this in the square crop rather than the 16:9, because square pulls the background much closer to her face.

Solid mid-tone wardrobe. No fine patterns, which will moiré. No white, which will blow out against a soft key. Nothing in the Smart Value violet family, or she will blend into the graphics on the cut at 0:08.

Audio

Lav plus a boom if you have both. Record the voice-over in the same room on the same chain as the on-camera audio.

If the V/O is recorded later on a different mic, the cut back to her face at 0:49 will sound like a different film, and the viewer will feel it even if they cannot name it.

Square
framing

Shoot 4K 16:9 or open gate and crop to 1:1 in post, with 1:1 guides on while framing. Compose so the square is the hero and the 16:9 is protection, not the other way round.

Extra
coverage

Cheap to capture on the day, expensive to go back for.

All three hook variants, three takes each, which is what lets you A/B the opening where the reach is decided. All three closer variants. Both sections at both sizes even with two cameras running. Ten seconds of her holding a neutral look with no dialogue, at both sizes. Thirty seconds of room tone.

Half a day covers all of it comfortably.

Cut rhythm

Something on screen should change every two to three seconds across the whole film. The animation beats already do this in the middle sections. In the on-camera sections the punch-in cuts are the only thing providing it, which is why they are not optional.

Options

Alternative hooks

Shoot all three. The opening is where reach is decided and the marginal cost on the day is about ten minutes.

Hook AScripted
What if you put 8% on your best-selling SKU? Someone in the room will say revenue goes up. Will it?

Keeps the what-if framing and turns it into a challenge rather than a riddle.

Hook B — the meeting

Drops the viewer into a deadline instead of a hypothetical. Strongest for Finance and Commercial, who live in that meeting.

Hook C — the cost of being right

Sharpest scroll-stopper, more confrontational. Test this one if reach is the priority.

Closer AScripted

Talent

What Mariam can and cannot say

Mariam Ghabrial is Marketing & PR Director at Marketeers, the sister company to Smart Value AI Solutions. She is not an RGM practitioner and the script must not imply otherwise. Senior commercial people can tell the difference inside one sentence.

The rule

She poses, the film proves. Both on-camera moments are questions. Everything that asserts anything about volume, switching or margin sits in the voice-over over graphics, where the evidence is on screen doing the arguing.

Keep that split in any rewrite and she has nothing to defend that is not hers.

Watch
one line

"Someone in the room will say revenue goes up" is observational, so it works from her. If the takes start to sound like she is claiming a seat in that room, change it to "You'll hear someone say revenue goes up." Decide on the day from the takes.

Voice-over

Recommend Mariam carries it herself. A second voice would make the hybrid structure more obvious, but across 58 seconds with only two on-camera moments it fragments the film into three people's opinions.

The cut from her face to the shelf graphic already supplies the rhythm change. One voice keeps it one argument. If a second voice is preferred, make it noticeably lower and steadier so the hand-off reads as deliberate.

Attribution

No lower third on the hook. The first three seconds are the most valuable real estate in the film, and a Marketeers title over a Smart Value film invites exactly the sister-company confusion worth avoiding.

The film posts from the Smart Value AI Solutions account, so credit her in the caption instead.

Delivery

Script the on-camera lines for structure, then give her keyword bullets rather than a teleprompter. The hook and the closer should sound argued, not recited. Record three takes of the hook at different energy levels.

Rationale

Why a straight 8% increase

Three other scenarios were on the table: pulling a promo, changing pack price architecture, and reacting to a competitor-led cut. The straight price increase wins mainly on comprehension speed.

A scrolling viewer gives you one to two seconds. "+8% on your best seller" is legible in that window with no decoding. "Pull the 25% promo" needs a beat of setup, and a pack architecture change needs several. In a film whose job is curiosity before product, the scenario that lands fastest wins even if it is not the most sophisticated question in the category.

It also maps cleanly onto the four outcomes. Pay it, trade down, switch brand, leave the category are the natural responses to a price going up. On a promo withdrawal or a competitor-led move those branches shift and the animation gets muddier.

Recommendation — shoot two films

The promo question is the better second film, and it sells a different product. "What if you pulled the 25% promo on your biggest SKU?" maps onto Promotion Planner rather than Price Optimizer.

It is the more painful question for most commercial teams and closer to what they are arguing about this quarter. Once the shelf animation exists, film two is largely a re-voice and a re-label. Shoot both on the same day.

Post

Production notes

Sound off
is default

LinkedIn's own research puts only about 37% of mobile B2B viewers listening with sound, and captioned video holds viewers roughly a third longer.

Burn in captions rather than relying on auto-captions. Every number in the middle two sections needs an on-screen label that carries the argument alone.

Aspect
ratio

1:1 square master for LinkedIn. Desktop is still a large share of LinkedIn impressions and crops 4:5 badly. Produce 9:16 only for the Instagram and TikTok cutdowns.

Pace

134 words across 58 seconds is deliberately unhurried. The pauses are part of the argument.

On-screen
numbers

No client result is cleared, so nothing on screen should be styled to look sourced. Moving bars, bending curves and resolving tiles, not precise figures with decimal points.

A number that looks like a claim and is not one is the fastest way to lose senior commercial people. The one validated claim available belongs in the caption, where it can be qualified.
