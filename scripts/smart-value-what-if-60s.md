# Price Optimizer — "What If" (60s social film)

Client: Smart Value AI Solutions
Product: Price Optimizer
On camera: Mariam Ghabrial, Marketing & PR Director, Marketeers
Posting from: Smart Value AI Solutions account
Format: hybrid. On camera → V/O with motion graphics → on camera close
Platform: LinkedIn primary. 1:1 square master, 9:16 cutdown for Instagram and TikTok
Spoken word count: 134. Target runtime: 58 seconds
Audience: Commercial/Sales, Finance and Marketing at FMCG brands. Europe and developing markets

---

## NAMING DECISION — READ FIRST

The brief called the subject "Smart Value Pricing Optimization." The company deck says something more precise: **Smart Value AI Solutions** is the company, **Price Optimizer** is the product that clients buy. Promotion Planner and AI-Enabled Market Research sit beside it.

The script now says **Price Optimizer** in the voice-over and puts **Smart Value AI Solutions** on the end card. Product in the voice, company on the card.

Two reasons. A viewer who wants this has to be able to ask for it by name, and "Smart Value Pricing Optimization" is not a thing they can ask for. And the film is about one decision, so naming the one product that makes that decision is sharper than naming the whole company and leaving them to find the right door.

If house policy is to lead with the parent brand at this stage, the line reverts cleanly to "Smart Value runs the question before you commit." Same eight words, same timing, no other change needed.

---

## POSITIONING ALIGNMENT

The company line is **"Decision support, powered by AI. Predict before you decide."** and the deck already says *"Decisions are tested through simulation, not analysed after the fact."*

That is the thesis of this film, which is a good sign. So the film should reinforce that line rather than compete with a second one.

Consequence: the end card carries **"Predict before you decide"**, the existing brand line, not a new one. Mariam's spoken "Simulate it first" is the film's in-the-moment version of the same idea. One line on screen, one in the mouth, same argument.

The earlier draft ended on "Before you change the price, simulate the decision." It is a decent line but it duplicates the brand line at lower strength. It moves to the post caption instead.

## THE SCRIPT

### 1. HOOK — ON CAMERA (0:00–0:08)

**MARIAM, ON CAMERA**
> "What if you put 8% on your best-selling SKU?
>
> Someone in the room will say revenue goes up.
>
> Will it?"

**DIRECTION**
Open mid-thought. No logo, no greeting, no name card.
Cut on "Will it?" to half a second of hard silence before the graphics start. Do not fill it with music.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT (0:00)**
`+8% on your best seller.`
Up on frame one, top third, so the film reads with the sound off.

---

### 2. COMPLICATE THE DECISION — V/O + ANIMATION (0:08–0:31)

**VOICE-OVER**
> "Depends who's standing in the aisle.
>
> Some pay it and never notice.
>
> Some drop to the smaller pack. You kept the shopper and lost the value.
>
> A few walk one shelf across to the competitor.
>
> And a few just stop buying the category.
>
> Same decision, four different answers, and your forecast only modelled one."

**DIRECTION**
Build one shelf graphic and hold it for the whole section. Everything happens on that shelf.

- "who's standing in the aisle" — the shelf draws in. One price tag flips 100 to 108.
- "Some pay it" — a shopper path runs straight to the SKU. Stays green. Volume bar barely moves.
- "smaller pack" — the path bends down the shelf to a smaller pack of the same brand. Volume holds, value drops. Label the drop.
- "one shelf across" — the path leaves the brand block into a competitor block and takes the competitor's colour. Let this beat land hardest. It is the expensive one.
- "stop buying the category" — the path fades out of frame. Brief drop in the mix.
- "four different answers" — all four paths replay at once, fast, over the same shelf. Four tiles resolve together: volume, revenue, margin, share.
- "only modelled one" — three tiles grey out. One stays lit.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT**
`Pays it` · `Trades down` · `Switches brand` · `Leaves the category`
Then: `One price change. Four P&Ls.`

---

### 3. PRICE OPTIMIZER — V/O + PRODUCT (0:31–0:49)

**VOICE-OVER**
> "Price Optimizer runs the question before you commit.
>
> Move the price. Watch volume, margin and the portfolio move with it.
>
> See where the switching goes. See where the shopper stops paying.
>
> Test it on screen, not in the market."

**DIRECTION**
Hand off from the illustrated shelf to real UI in the same colour language, so it reads as the same argument rather than a new chapter.

- "runs the question" — the +8% from the hook appears as a scenario row. Same number, loop closed.
- "Move the price" — a slider moves, outputs recalculate live. Do not cut during the recalculation. The whole point is that the numbers move together.
- "the portfolio move with it" — pull back to the full SKU list. Two or three neighbouring SKUs move the other way. That shows cannibalisation without narrating it.
- "where the switching goes" — switching flows between brands.
- "where the shopper stops paying" — willingness to pay curve, threshold marked. Land on the cliff edge.
- "not in the market" — +4%, +8% and +12% stack side by side with different winners. Hold two seconds. This is the money frame.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT**
`Simulate. Then decide.`

**SCREENS TO SOURCE FOR THE EDIT**
1. Scenario row set to +8% on a single SKU
2. Price slider mid-move with outputs recalculating
3. Full portfolio view with neighbouring SKUs reacting
4. Switching or source-of-volume view between brands
5. Willingness to pay curve with the threshold visible
6. Three scenarios compared side by side

Capture at the highest resolution available, with real cursor movement rather than static screenshots. A still screen pretending to be a live one is visible on a 2x timeline.

`Smart Value AI Solutions` (logo)
`Price Optimizer`
`Predict before you decide.`
Small, under the line: `Models validated against real market outcomes.`

**ON THE 92% FIGURE**
The deck carries a stronger proof point: *92% confidence on an actual pricing recommendation.* I have deliberately kept the number off the end card and put the softer validation line there instead.

A specific figure on screen for two seconds invites "92% of what, measured how" from exactly the people this film is aimed at, and there is no room to answer them. Unqualified, it reads as marketing. Qualified, it needs a sentence. So it goes in the post caption, where there is room, and where the people who care enough to ask are already reading.

Confirm the exact wording with whoever owns that claim before it appears anywhere.

## SHOT LIST AND CAMERA ANGLES

Only two on-camera setups in this film, so both have to work hard. The single biggest retention decision is the two-camera setup below.

### Shoot on two cameras

**A-cam.** Medium close-up, chest up. Roughly 50mm full-frame equivalent. Eye height or a degree or two below. Eyeline direct into the lens.

**B-cam.** Close-up, shoulders up. Roughly 85mm equivalent, positioned 20 to 30 degrees off the A-cam axis, matched in height.

This gives the editor a clean cut anywhere without a jump cut, and it is what makes the punch-in below possible. If only one camera is available, shoot every line twice at both sizes and match the performance.

### Setup A — the hook (0:00–0:08)

**Shot 1, A-cam, MCU, direct to lens.**
Direct address is doing the work here. She is asking the viewer a question, so any off-axis eyeline turns it into an interview and the question stops being aimed at anyone.

Compose her on the left third. The negative space on the right is where `+8% on your best seller.` sits. In a square crop this matters more than you expect, because centred framing plus a text overlay collides.

Camera locked off, or a very slow push in of two or three percent across the eight seconds. Imperceptible, but it adds forward pressure. No handheld. Handheld reads as vlog, and this film is asking for commercial authority.

Aperture around f/2.8 to f/4. Enough separation to lift her off the background without it looking like a portrait session.

**Shot 2, B-cam, CU, on "Will it?"**
Cut to the tighter, off-axis frame for the last two words.

This punch-in is the most valuable two seconds of coverage in the film. Drop-off concentrates around the five-second mark, and a hard size change right at that point resets attention without adding a word. Same reason the cut lands on the question rather than after it.

### Setup B — the close (0:49–0:58)

**A-cam, medium shot, waist up, direct to lens.**
Wider than the hook, same lighting, same background. Pulling back at the end signals resolution. Going tighter would read as escalation, which is the wrong note for a line about thinking before acting.

Consider one cut to the B-cam on "Simulate it first," but only if the take supports it. If her delivery is strong, stay wide and let it play. A cut there can look like you did not trust the line.

### Lighting

Soft key at about 45 degrees, large source, short side. Keying the far side of her face from camera gives shape and reads more serious than flat frontal light.

Background one and a half to two stops under. A window or practicals behind her at f/2.8 give clean separation.

Avoid a white cyc. This audience recognises white-cyc corporate video as an ad within a second and scrolls past it.

### Background and wardrobe

Real environment, defocused. Office or meeting room, not a branded backdrop. Nothing readable behind her head, and check this in the square crop rather than the 16:9, because square pulls the background much closer to her face.

Solid mid-tone wardrobe. No fine patterns, which will moiré. No white, which will blow out against a soft key. Nothing in the Smart Value purple family, or she will blend into the graphics on the cut at 0:08.

### Framing for square

Shoot 4K 16:9 or open gate and crop to 1:1 in post, with 1:1 guides on while framing. Compose so the square is the hero and the 16:9 is protection, not the other way round.

Give more headroom than instinct suggests. Square punishes tight headroom faster than widescreen does.

### Audio

Lav plus a boom if you have both. Record the voice-over in the same room on the same chain as the on-camera audio. If the V/O is recorded later on a different mic, the cut back to her face at 0:49 will sound like a different film and the viewer will feel it even if they cannot name it.

### Coverage to shoot beyond the script

Cheap to capture on the day, expensive to go back for.

- All three hook variants, three takes each. Enough to A/B the opening, which is where the reach is decided.
- All three closer variants.
- Both on-camera sections at both sizes even with two cameras running, as insurance.
- Ten seconds of her holding a neutral look, no dialogue, at both sizes. Useful for a still frame and for covering an edit problem.
- Room tone, thirty seconds.

Half a day covers all of it comfortably.

### Editing note on pace

Something on screen should change every two to three seconds across the whole film. The animation beats already do this in sections 2 and 3. In the on-camera sections, the punch-in cuts are the only thing providing it, which is why they are not optional.

## ALTERNATIVE HOOKS

**A. As written.** "What if you put 8% on your best-selling SKU? Someone in the room will say revenue goes up. Will it?"
Keeps the what-if framing and turns it into a challenge rather than a riddle.

**B. The meeting.** "Someone has already asked for 8% on your biggest SKU. You have a week to answer."
Drops the viewer into a deadline instead of a hypothetical. Strongest for Finance and Commercial, who live in that meeting.

**C. The cost of being right.** "Eight percent on your best seller. The model says yes. The model has never met your shopper."
Sharpest scroll-stopper, more confrontational. Test this one if reach is the priority.

## ALTERNATIVE CLOSERS

**A. As written.** "So the question was never 'can we raise the price.' It's 'what happens when we do.' Simulate it first."

**B. Plainer.** "Everyone can raise a price. The job is knowing what it costs you."
Drops the isn't/it's construction if the delivery feels too written on the day.

## CASTING NOTE — MARIAM ON CAMERA

Mariam Ghabrial is Marketing & PR Director at Marketeers, the sister company to Smart Value AI Solutions. She is not an RGM practitioner and the script must not imply otherwise.

**She poses, the film proves.** Both on-camera moments are questions. Everything that asserts anything about volume, switching or margin sits in the voice-over over graphics, where the evidence is on screen doing the arguing. Keep that split in any rewrite and she has nothing to defend that is not hers.

**Watch one line.** "Someone in the room will say revenue goes up" is observational, so it works from her. If the takes start to sound like she is claiming a seat in that room, change it to "You'll hear someone say revenue goes up." Decide on the day.

**Voice-over: recommend Mariam carries it.** A second voice would make the hybrid structure more obvious, but across 58 seconds with only two on-camera moments it fragments the film into three people's opinions. The cut from her face to the shelf graphic already supplies the rhythm change. One voice keeps it one argument. If a second voice is preferred, make it noticeably lower and steadier so the hand-off reads as deliberate.

**Attribution.** No lower third on the hook. The first three seconds are the most valuable real estate in the film, and a Marketeers title over a Smart Value film invites exactly the sister-company confusion worth avoiding. The film posts from the Smart Value AI Solutions account, so credit her in the caption.

## SCENARIO CHOICE — WHY +8% PRICE INCREASE

Three other scenarios were on the table. Keeping the straight price increase, mainly for comprehension speed.

A scrolling viewer gives you one to two seconds. "+8% on your best seller" is legible in that window with no decoding. "Pull the 25% promo" needs a beat of setup and a pack architecture change needs several. In a film whose job is curiosity before product, the scenario that lands fastest wins even if it is not the most sophisticated question in the category.

It also maps cleanly onto the four outcomes in section 2. Pay it, trade down, switch brand, leave the category are the natural responses to a price going up. On a promo withdrawal or a competitor-led move those branches shift and the animation gets muddier.

**The promo question is the better second film,** and it maps onto Promotion Planner rather than Price Optimizer, so it sells a different product off the same shoot. "What if you pulled the 25% promo on your biggest SKU?" is the more painful question for most commercial teams and closer to what they are arguing about this quarter. Once the shelf animation exists it is largely a re-voice and a re-label. Recommend shooting both on the same day.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Sound off is the default.** LinkedIn's own research puts only about 37% of mobile B2B viewers listening with sound, and captioned video holds viewers roughly a third longer. Burn in captions rather than relying on auto-captions. Every number in sections 2 and 3 needs an on-screen label that carries the argument alone.

**Aspect ratio.** 1:1 square master for LinkedIn. Desktop is still a large share of LinkedIn impressions and crops 4:5 badly. Produce 9:16 only for the Instagram and TikTok cutdowns.

**Delivery.** Script the on-camera lines for structure, then give Mariam keyword bullets rather than a teleprompter. The hook and the closer should sound argued, not recited. Record three takes of the hook at different energy levels.

**Pace.** 134 words across 58 seconds is deliberately unhurried. The pauses are part of the argument.

**On-screen numbers stay directional.** No client result is cleared, so nothing on screen should be styled to look sourced. Moving bars, bending curves and resolving tiles, not precise figures with decimal points. A number that looks like a claim and is not one is the fastest way to lose senior commercial people. The one validated claim available belongs in the caption, where it can be qualified.
